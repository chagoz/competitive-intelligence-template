# Flash Report Template

*The structure for a single-topic special edition. Used when findings consolidate around
one question with direct business consequences, and the weekly digest shape would bury
the point across eight sections.*

*What this document owns: section order, the numbering rule, the strategy brief rule, and
the data-sufficiency gate for single-topic editions. What it does not own: voice,
formatting, the emoji system and the mandatory inline markers, which all live in
`language-guidelines.md` Part 1. Scoring, tier logic and source honesty rules live in
`analysis-rules.md`.*

*Created August 18, 2026 · v3*

## When to use this instead of the weekly digest

Three conditions, all of them:

The findings consolidate around one question rather than spreading across competitors.
The question has a live business consequence, usually a decision someone is about to make.
The research is mission-depth rather than a weekly scan, so a competitor-by-competitor
walk would repeat the same finding nine times.

If only the first is true, it is still a weekly digest with a strong lead story.

## Numbering

A Flash Report is named, not numbered. It does not take a digest number, and it does not
break the weekly sequence.

## Voice

Digest speed and directness, one notch more measured. The register is "here is what we
found and why it matters" rather than a routine quick take. Higher stakes means the proof
sits closer to the claim than in a weekly edition.

## Section order, with headers

| # | Section | Header |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening subtitle | Grey italic, one line, states the scope of the research pass in numbers |
| 2 | Three things to remember | `## 🎯 Three things to remember` |
| 3 | The strategy brief | `## 🗺️ [N] ways to play [topic]` |
| 4 | The vocabulary, or the map | `## 🗣️ The vocabulary` |
| 5 | Talk versus implement | `## 🤷 Talk versus implement` |
| 6 | What the evidence shows | `## 🔧 What users actually say` |
| 7 | Where nobody is playing | `## 🕳️ Where nobody is playing` |
| 8 | Regulation watch | `## 📊 Regulation watch` |
| 9 | What is hot elsewhere | `## 💥 What is hot elsewhere` |
| 10 | Open questions | `## 🔍 Open questions and what we could not confirm` |
| 11 | Two closing lines | Grey italic |
| 12 | Credits | Grey italic, including the size of the research pass |

Sections 8 and 9 are conditional. Everything else is standing.

**Why the gap section sits at 7 and not at the top.** A Quarterly Review leads with impact
and closes with proof. A Flash Report does the opposite, because the reader needs the map
before the gap means anything. Do not reorder these two out of habit.

**Why section 10 is not optional.** A single-topic edition claims more depth than a weekly
does, so it owes a clearer account of its own limits.

## Inline markers, mandatory

*From `language-guidelines.md` Part 1. This is the part most likely to be dropped, and the
part that carries the most reading value in a document built around verdicts.*

- Every verdict in section 5 carries both axes with both markers: `🔧 Implement · 🔴 High 5/5`.
- Every verdict cell in any table: `🗣️ Talk` or `🔧 Implement`, never the bare word.
- 🔥 on the sharpest finding.
- ✏️ on any correction made after publication, with a dated callout near the top explaining
  what changed and what the superseded reading was. Keep the old reading visible rather than
  deleting it.
- A grey italic score legend sits directly above the first section that uses the markers.

## The strategy brief rule

Sits directly after the three things, directly before the vocabulary table. It is the map;
the vocabulary is the detailed version of the same map, so they belong next to each other
in that order.

**It is competitor strategy only.** No comparison to our own position, no differentiator
framing, no implicit product pitch. The reader is being handed the shape of the market so
they can think with it. Our own position, where it belongs at all, belongs in the "So what
for us" callout further down, clearly marked as our read rather than as market fact.

**Three to five strategies, no more.** Fewer than three means the market is not actually
segmented on this topic and the section adds nothing. More than five and it stops being a
fast read, which was the whole point.

**A company may appear in more than one row, and should when it genuinely runs two plays.**
Do not force a company into a single box for tidiness. Where a company appears twice, say
so in a grey italic line under the table, because the doubling is usually a finding in
itself.

**Close with one or two observations, not a summary.** The table already summarises. The
paragraphs underneath should name a pattern the table cannot show on its own, for example
that two design choices readers assume are linked are in fact independent.

## Data-sufficiency gate

A Flash Report claims depth on one question, so thin evidence damages it more than it would
damage a weekly digest.

Before writing, check that the evidence base actually supports a single-topic edition. If
the answer to the question rests on fewer than three independently sourced findings, it is
a digest lead story, not a Flash Report.

Where coverage is uneven across companies, state the unevenness in the section where it
appears, not in a footnote. Absence of evidence gets reported as a finding in its own
right, with the search trail, rather than being left as a blank row.

## Heading structure

Maximum two heading levels. A Flash Report is decision support, not reference
documentation, so every heading lands in the table of contents and more levels make it
unreadable. Use dividers and bold paragraph titles for structure below H2.
