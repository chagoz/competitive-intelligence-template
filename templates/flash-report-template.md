# Flash Report Template

*The structure for a single-topic special edition. Use this when findings consolidate around one question with real business consequences, and the weekly digest shape would bury the point across eight sections. Mirrored in Claude Project knowledge as `flash-report-template.md`, so both copies need updating together.*

## What this document owns

*Written down so nothing gets defined twice in two places.*

**Owns:** section order, the numbering rule, the strategy brief rule, and the data-sufficiency gate for single-topic editions.

**Does not own:** voice, formatting, colour registers, the pre-flight checklist, and the symbol system, which all live in `language-guidelines.md` Part 1. Scoring, tier logic and source honesty rules live in `analysis-rules.md`. Weekly cadence structure lives in `digest-template-structure.md`.

---

## When to use this instead of the weekly digest

Three conditions, and all three have to be true.

The findings consolidate around one question rather than spreading across competitors.

The question has a live business consequence, usually a decision someone is about to make.

The research is mission-depth rather than a weekly scan, so a competitor-by-competitor walk would repeat the same finding nine times.

If only the first is true, it is still a weekly digest with a strong lead story.

---

## Numbering and voice

**Numbering.** A Flash Report is named, not numbered. It does not take a digest number, and it does not break the weekly sequence.

**Voice.** Digest speed and directness, one notch more measured. The register is "here is what we found and why it matters" rather than a routine quick take. Higher stakes means the proof sits closer to the claim than it does in a weekly edition.

## Section order

*Twelve blocks, in this order. Sections 8 and 9 are conditional. Everything else is standing.*

| # | Section | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Opening subtitle | Grey italic, one line. States the scope of the research pass in numbers, so the reader knows the weight of what follows before reading it. |
| 2 | 🎯 Three things to remember | Same rule as the digest. Confirmed at the editorial gate, never self-selected. |
| 3 | 🖼️ The strategy brief | One table naming the distinct approaches competitors take to the topic. See the rule below. |
| 4 | 🖼️ The vocabulary, or the map | One table establishing what the topic actually means across the market, company by company. The section that only exists in a Flash Report, and the reason the format exists. Same symbol as the strategy brief above, both are "establishing the map before reporting," the master symbol system already treats these as one family rather than two. |
| 5 | 🗣️🔧 Talk versus implement | Both axes shown. Companies with nothing to assess are named in grey, one line, never omitted. An absence is a finding. |
| 6 | 🔧 What the evidence shows | Layer 2, dated and source-tagged. Where the review base is thin, say so in the same breath as the finding. |
| 7 | ➖ Where nobody is playing | The gap section, usually the actual insight. |
| 8 | 📊 Regulation watch | Only if the topic has a regulatory dimension. Plain-language gloss on first mention. |
| 9 | 💥 What is hot elsewhere | Carried threads and anything outside the topic. Keeps the edition from dropping the weekly rhythm entirely. Short. |
| 10 | 🔍 Open questions and what we could not confirm | Named gaps with the search trail. Not optional. |
| 11 | Two closing lines | Grey italic. |
| 12 | Credits | Including the size of the research pass. |

**Why the gap section sits at 7 and not at the top.** A Quarterly Review leads with impact and closes with proof. A Flash Report does the opposite, because the reader needs the map before the gap means anything. Do not reorder these two out of habit.

**Why section 10 is not optional.** A single-topic edition claims more depth than a weekly does, so it owes a clearer account of its own limits.

## Symbol system

*Every symbol here is defined in `language-guidelines.md` Part 1, this section only says which of those symbols apply to a Flash Report specifically, it does not redefine any of them. If a Flash Report needs a symbol not listed below, check Part 1 before inventing one, the same mistake that put the emoji rule in the wrong place once already is easy to repeat.*

**Section headers, in order:** 🎯 Three things to remember · 🖼️ The strategy brief · 🖼️ The vocabulary · 🗣️🔧 Talk versus implement (the paired symbols, not a new one, since this section covers both axes together) · 🔧 What the evidence shows · ➖ Where nobody is playing · 📊 Regulation watch · 💥 What is hot elsewhere · 🔍 Open questions.

**Inline markers are mandatory and are the first thing to get dropped under time pressure.** A verdict without its marker is an unfinished verdict.

| Marker | Means | Required on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 🗣️ | Talk, announced or claimed, nothing verified as shipped | Every verdict cell and every verdict line |
| 🔧 | Implement, verified as running or shipped | Every verdict cell and every verdict line |
| 🔴 🟡 ⚪ | Threat High 4-5, Moderate 3, Low 1-2 | Every two-axis score, shown together with the verdict marker |
| 🔥 | The sharpest finding in the document | Once, at most |
| ✏️ | Correction made after publication | Dated callout near the top, superseded reading kept visible |

**Format for a two-axis score:** `🔧 Implement · 🔴 High 5/5`. Both axes, both markers, always together, never collapsed into one number.

*A grey italic score legend sits directly above the first section that uses these markers. Reading order: know the code, then read the verdicts.*

## The strategy brief rule

*The fastest read in the document and the one most people will actually finish.*

**Placement.** Directly after the three things, directly before the vocabulary table. The brief is the map, the vocabulary is the detailed version of the same map, so they belong next to each other in that order.

**It is competitor strategy only.** No comparison to our own position, no differentiator framing, no implicit product pitch. The reader is being handed the shape of the market so they can think with it. Our own position, where it belongs at all, belongs in the "So what for us" callout further down, clearly marked as our read rather than as market fact.

**Three to five strategies, no more.** Fewer than three means the market is not actually segmented on this topic and the section adds nothing. More than five and it stops being a fast read, which was the whole point.

**A company may appear in more than one row, and should when it genuinely runs two plays.** Do not force a company into a single box for tidiness. Where a company appears twice, say so in a grey italic line under the table, because the doubling is usually a finding in itself.

**Close with one or two observations, not a summary.** The table already summarises. The paragraphs underneath should name a pattern the table cannot show on its own, for example that two design choices readers assume are linked are in fact independent.

## Data-sufficiency gate

*A Flash Report claims depth on one question, so thin evidence damages it more than it would damage a weekly digest.*

Before writing, check that the evidence base actually supports a single-topic edition. If the answer to the question rests on fewer than three independently sourced findings, it is a digest lead story, not a Flash Report.

Where coverage is uneven across companies, state the unevenness in the section where it appears, not in a footnote.

Absence of evidence gets reported as a finding in its own right, with the search trail, rather than left as a blank row.

---

## Heading structure

Maximum two heading levels in the report itself. A Flash Report is decision support, not reference documentation, so every heading lands in the table of contents and more levels make it unreadable. Use dividers and bold paragraph titles for structure below H2.

*This template page is the exception. It is reference documentation rather than decision support, so it can afford more structure than the reports it describes.*
